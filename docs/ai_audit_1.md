# AI Novelty and Feasibility Audit

## AI Critique Summary

The proposed project, **“Does Quantization Recovery Generalize to Tool-Calling Reliability?”**, is a strong and technically meaningful empirical study. Its novelty is real, but it should be described precisely. The project does not introduce a new quantization algorithm, LoRA method, or knowledge-distillation objective. Instead, its main contribution is evaluating whether an existing recovery approach—2-bit quantization followed by LoRA recovery through knowledge distillation—generalizes beyond conventional language-model benchmarks to structured tool calling. This is a narrower contribution than proposing a new compression method, but it addresses an important and underexplored question: whether recovering general model quality also restores the exact behavioral reliability required by tool-using systems.

The strongest part of the proposal is its capability-transfer framing. Most quantization and recovery studies emphasize perplexity, multiple-choice accuracy, or general instruction-following performance. These measurements may hide failures that become critical during tool use, such as selecting the wrong function, producing invalid JSON, omitting required arguments, or assigning incorrect argument values. The project therefore evaluates not merely whether recovery improves a compressed model, but whether the improvement transfers across different capability types. This distinction gives the study a clear research gap and prevents it from being only another benchmark reproduction.

The comparison between general-domain recovery data and tool-domain recovery data provides a second meaningful contribution. By holding the recovery method fixed and varying the data domain, the project can test whether tool-calling reliability requires domain-matched recovery examples. This is practically relevant because model developers may assume that general knowledge-distillation data is sufficient to restore all capabilities after aggressive compression. A result showing unequal recovery across capabilities—or improved recovery from tool-domain data—would provide useful evidence for designing compressed agentic models.

## Red-Ocean and Oversaturation Risks

The broader areas of LLM quantization, LoRA adaptation, and knowledge distillation are highly saturated. Many recent projects already compare quantization levels, adapter ranks, benchmark accuracy, latency, and memory usage. A project framed simply as “quantize a model and recover its accuracy with LoRA” would have weak novelty and would compete directly with a large body of existing work. Similarly, demonstrating that supervised fine-tuning improves tool calling would not be novel by itself.

The proposal avoids most of this red-ocean risk through its controlled evaluation design. Its value comes from analyzing *where recovery transfers and where it fails*, rather than claiming a new recovery recipe. However, the final report must preserve this distinction. Claims such as “a novel quantization-recovery method” would overstate the contribution. A defensible claim is that the work provides a systematic evaluation of capability-specific recovery under aggressive quantization, with particular attention to structured tool-calling reliability and recovery-data domain.

Another risk is that the study could become a collection of benchmark scores without producing a clear conclusion. The proposed failure decomposition helps address this by separating tool selection, formatting, argument inclusion, and argument-value errors. The Suite A/B/C structure is also valuable because it distinguishes general generation or ranking degradation from genuine tool-calling failures. These controls make the results more interpretable than a single aggregate accuracy number.

## Feasibility and Overall Assessment

The project is feasible for a semester-scale graduate deep-learning project because it uses existing base models, quantization methods, LoRA training, and established evaluation datasets rather than requiring full model pretraining. The experimental scope is still ambitious, especially when combining multiple models, recovery conditions, LoRA ranks, random seeds, and evaluation suites. The primary model and essential comparisons should therefore remain the required core, while additional-model replication should be treated as optional if compute or time becomes limited.

Overall, this is a strong and credible proposal with **high feasibility and moderate-to-strong empirical novelty**. Its novelty is not foundational algorithmic novelty; it lies in the research question, controlled capability-transfer evaluation, domain-matched recovery comparison, and detailed analysis of structured failures. If executed reproducibly and reported without overstating the method contribution, it would be an excellent graduate course project and could support a workshop-style empirical paper.
